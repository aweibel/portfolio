Overview

Most designers can explain a project well out loud. When they write it down, the numbers, the false starts and the disagreements with other teams tend to get left out, and those are the parts hiring managers look for.

The skill works in three steps.

  1. It writes a draft from what you said. You can talk, dictate or paste notes. It sorts everything into ten sections, writes in the first person, and marks anything it doesn't know with a placeholder instead of guessing.
  2. It asks about what's missing. It rates each section and asks up to five questions at a time, starting with impact: the number before, the number after, the time period, and where you might find them.
  3. It adds the case study to your portfolio. It finds your existing case study page, template or doc, fits the sections into it, and keeps the page marked as coming soon until the gaps are filled.

The structure comes from people who hire designers: a Nielsen Norman Group survey of 204 UX hiring managers, the design hiring guides from Etsy and Google, a former head of product design, a design hiring manager and a former recruiter. Links are in the reference section.

Install

Claude Code
Unzip it into your skills folder and it will be available in every project.
unzip case-study.skill -d ~/.claude/skills/
You can also unzip it into a single project's .claude/skills/ folder, for example your portfolio repo.
Claude apps
If your plan supports custom skills, upload case-study.skill in the skills settings.

Use

You don't need a special command. Asking for a case study, a portfolio piece, a project write-up, a portfolio entry or a work sample will start it, and so will asking what an existing case study is missing. You can also type /case-study.

Examples

A summary that works

This example comes from the skill's own guidance, and its numbers are made up. A good summary covers the problem, what you did and the result in two sentences.

Writing about a disagreement

Sales wanted to keep the wizard. Our VP of Sales thought the wizard helped close deals because it looked good in demos. Instead of arguing about taste, I showed her five session recordings of admins quitting at step 9, and suggested keeping a demo mode for sales calls. We gave the checklist to new accounts first so we could compare the two, and once the numbers came in, Sales started using the checklist in their own demos.

Hiring managers say a project where nothing went wrong reads as edited, so the skill gives disagreements and failed ideas their own sections.

How it marks what it doesn't know

  • METRIC median time from signup to go-live, before and after, over the first 90 days
  • ASK who pushed back on removing the wizard, and why?
  • IMAGE the old and new setup screens side by side, with the removed steps circled

You'll see these in the draft. They're hidden on the published page, so a recruiter never sees an unfinished sentence.

A question it asked

"You said setup got way faster and implementation calls dropped a lot. Do you remember roughly how long it took a new customer to go live before and after, and how many implementation calls or hours a typical setup needed before and after, over what period? You mentioned rolling the checklist out to new customers first so you could compare. That comparison is probably the best place to look, along with whatever analytics tool you used, launch or QBR decks, the implementation team's call logs, or asking Dana. If exact numbers are gone, would rough wording like "from weeks to days" be accurate?"

From the example further down the page. It uses details from the notes, asks for every part of the number, and suggests where to find it.

Docs

The ten sections

SectionWhat goes in it
SummaryA one or two sentence summary of the problem, what I did and the result, plus three key takeaways
ContextWho it's for, what was at stake for the business, why it happened then, constraints and scope
RoleWhat I owned, what the team did, partners by function
ResearchResearch methods, the people it was designed for, something a user said, and what the findings changed
ProcessThe options explored, why one was chosen, and how it was tested
SetbacksWhat didn't work, ideas that were dropped, changes of direction
DisagreementsA real disagreement, both sides of it, how it was settled and what was given up
SolutionThe final design and the details you cared most about
ImpactResults for the business and for users, with numbers before and after, the time period, and how they were measured
Looking backWhat I learned and what I'd do differently

How it behaves

  • It doesn't make anything up. Every number, quote and result has to come from what you said, and a script checks each number in the draft against your notes.
  • It checks things you weren't sure about. If you say "I think it was fourteen steps", it uses the number and adds it to a list to confirm with you.
  • It asks five questions at most per round. You can answer them out loud, in any order.
  • It keeps your notes private. Your raw notes are saved in .case-study/, away from anything that gets published. That matters if they mention a client under NDA.
  • It handles NDAs. A named client becomes something like "a large US insurer", and the shot list tells you to recreate screens rather than use screenshots.
  • It writes like a person. No em dashes, no arrows and no stock phrases, since the case study goes out under your name.
  • It uses your layout. It fits the ten sections into your own template and saves the result in case-study.config.json, so your next case study goes in without the same questions.
