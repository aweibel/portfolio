# Rubric: what reviewers check

Use this to score each section **complete / thin / missing** and to spot red flags. It distils what people who actually hire product designers say they look for.

## Sources

Only practitioners and hiring-side sources (no SEO or vendor content):

- **NN/g survey of 204 UX hiring managers**: [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- **Etsy's design recruiter and hiring managers**: [Product designer portfolio & case study prep guide](https://extfiles.etsy.com/CareersPage/ProductDesign.pdf)
- **Google's UX hiring team**: [UX design portfolio tips](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/ux_design_portfolio_tips_19.pdf)
- **Jeff Zych**, ex-Head of Product Design at LaunchDarkly, who reviewed hundreds of case studies: [Ingredients for a great product design case study](https://jlzych.com/2022/10/24/ingredients-for-a-great-product-design-case-study/)
- **Taylor Palmer**, hiring manager: [5 principles of exceptional case studies](https://www.uxtools.co/blog/5-principles-of-exceptional-case-studies-in-ux-portfolios)
- **Emily Backes**, former technical recruiter and now a senior product designer: [What I actually look at in a portfolio review](https://emilybackes.design/post/what-i-actually-look-at-in-a-portfolio-review)

## Per section

| Section | Complete when… | Source signal |
|---|---|---|
| summary | The problem is restatable in one sentence. The TL;DR runs problem → action → result. The quick facts are filled in. There are 3 takeaways. | Palmer's "refined lead". Backes: about 10 seconds on the first screen. Google: "know the exact problem and solution". |
| context | It covers the users, the business stakes, why now, the constraints and the scope, with no more than the reader needs. | Zych's "targeted context". NN/g: problem, constraints, timeline. |
| role | "I" is used for owned work. Partners are named by function. Team size is given. | Google and Etsy: "clarify your role… who you partnered with". Backes: a vague "we" is a red flag. |
| research | It names the methods and participants, describes real user situations or personas, includes at least one real quote, and shows each insight leading to a decision. | NN/g: how research shaped the design. Etsy: quant and qual insights → decisions. |
| process | It shows the alternatives considered, why one won, and how it was validated, with captioned visuals. | Zych: alternatives and why. NN/g: "don't just show me the finished product." |
| setbacks | It names at least one dropped idea, failure or pivot, and what changed because of it. | NN/g: dropped concepts and obstacles. Etsy: "What didn't work?" Palmer: "Show the mess." |
| tensions | It names a specific disagreement, describes both sides fairly, explains how it was resolved, and states the trade-off. | Backes: "a project with no friction reads as edited." Google: cross-functional collaboration. |
| solution | It shows the final design, connects it to the problem, and shows craft details. | Zych: craft and attention to detail. Google: visual style reflects craft. |
| impact | It has at least one metric with baseline → result, a timeframe and a measurement method, plus a qualitative signal, plus context. | Etsy: "How you measured the outcome." Zych: business and customer, quant and qual, no metrics theater. Backes: a specific number earns a second read. |
| retro | It includes a concrete lesson and a concrete "would do differently". | NN/g: learnings. Etsy: "What did you learn?" |

## Red flags (fix or ask about)

- A vague "we" with no stated personal contribution.
- Process without outcomes or numbers.
- Only final screens, with no alternatives or in-progress work.
- No friction: no setbacks and no tensions.
- Generic headings ("Research", "Ideation") instead of headings that say something.
- Artifacts (personas, sticky notes, journey maps) that don't explain any decision.
- A wall of text with no bold lead-ins, lists or captions.
- Any fact, number or quote that the user did not say. This is the worst flag: remove it and replace it with a placeholder.
- Confidential names or data when the user mentioned an NDA.

## Calibrating to seniority

For senior, staff or principal designers and design leaders, reviewers weight these more heavily:
- strategy and problem framing
- influence without authority
- the depth of the tensions section
- systems thinking
- multiplying others (mentoring, design systems, process)

Push harder on tensions, impact on the business, and decisions the user drove. Push less on step-by-step process.
